Mostly yes. It's calm, and I don't think it reads as bitter overall. The specifics (the email bit, your mom and music school) are concrete and not exaggerated, and that's what keeps it from sounding petty. The mom paragraph is the strongest part, and "That was a choice" is exactly the right size.

But a few spots stop being calm and turn into a verdict, and I suspect that's part of what's making you pause.

**"I don't think you did any of this to hurt me. I really don't. I think you did it because you could."** This is the line I'd look at hardest. The first two sentences are generous and the third undoes them. "Because you could" says they had power over you and used it, which is harsh, and "I really don't" reads like protesting too much. Your ex will hear the third sentence, not the first two. It's also a claim about their motive that they can't answer, since you've said you don't want to talk. If you want to stay calm and honest, say what you know rather than what you think they intended: "I don't know why you did it. I know it landed, and I didn't tell you then."

**"It wasn't even funny."** This is the one line that reads petty. It shifts from "this hurt me" to "you were bad at this." Cut it. The paragraph is stronger without it.

**The three disclaimers.** "Isn't meant to start a conversation," "not looking for an apology," "don't want to talk about it." One is fine. Three sounds like someone bracing, and it can make the message feel like a door being shut after handing them a list.

On the pause, which I think is the more interesting question: you've spent two weeks on tone and you still stop, so I doubt tone is the real issue. I only have this page to go on, so these are guesses:

- The message says you need nothing from them, but "I saw it clearly" and "that was a choice" are things people write when they want to be acknowledged. If part of you is hoping for some kind of response, sending a "please don't respond" message might feel wrong in a way you can't name yet.
- You described the breakup as "just wasn't working out," but the letter describes someone using something painful of yours as a weapon. Those are two different stories. Maybe you're less finished with this than "say it calmly and move on" assumes.
- Or it's simpler: sending it reopens contact, even one-way, and you're not sure you want that.

One way to test it: imagine the three likely responses, which are silence, a defensive reply, and a real apology. Whichever one you'd mind most tells you what you're actually sending this for.

Your friend may be right that it's fine to send. But fine to send and ready to send aren't the same thing. If you cut those two lines and still hesitate, I'd treat the hesitation as information rather than something to push through. Writing it out may have already done a lot of the work. I also don't know your ex. If they tend to get defensive, or to reply no matter what you ask, that changes how this lands, and you know that better than I do.
