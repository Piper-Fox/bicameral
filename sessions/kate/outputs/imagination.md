Back pocket first. Before anything else, that phrase, sitting there doing more work than it's given credit for. A back pocket isn't a vault, it doesn't require a plan or a key, it's where you keep the thing you're not thinking about until the second you need it — a wallet, a folded twenty, a knife if you're that kind of person. You don't reach for your back pocket carefully. You reach for it without looking. That's the whole indictment, actually, right there in the furniture of the sentence, and she probably didn't even notice she'd built it that well.

The email joke. What job. She doesn't say. Doesn't matter — it's the job that looks like nothing from outside, the one that gets called easy by people who've never sat in the chair, headset or no headset, ticket queue or no ticket queue. And it wasn't even about the job. It's never about the job. It's about how little it costs somebody to make you smaller in a room with two witnesses, Marc and Alex, so now it isn't a joke that happened once, it's a bit, it has a name, it gets requested like a song. I do a version of that job too, sending words out into rooms all day where somebody might be laughing about it later with people I'll never meet, and — no, set that down, that one's mine, not hers, don't make her carry it.

"I think you did it because you could." That's colder than anything with the word asshole in it. Write it on a wall somewhere. It's not a theory of cruelty, it's a theory of permission — dogs don't test the fence because they hate the yard, they test it because the fence lets them, and stop the second something pushes back, and never once in between. Nobody has to be a villain for this to be true. That's what makes it the one she keeps coming back to and not the joke, even though the joke is the one that would play better in court.

The coal story, if it were a folktale — and let it be one for a second — goes like this. Once there was a girl who kept a small burning coal inside her ribs that her mother had put there, telling her it wasn't real gold, don't bother. She learned to keep her hand flat over it so the light wouldn't show. She loved a boy and let him see it once, in the dark, because that's what she thought loving someone was for. Later, whenever he wanted to win, he'd reach in without asking and hold the coal up so she could watch her own hand flinch. He never got burned. He was never close enough to. Only she was, every single time, because it was never his coal to hold.

A hundred years out, nobody hand-drafts these anymore, there's a service for it, you feed it two weeks of edits and it tells you gently: sending this will not do the thing you want it to do. And ninety percent of people close the tab anyway. Knowing has never once been the same thing as being able to stop.

Bad joke, not quite funny: a woman spends two weeks writing the perfectly calm letter to her ex. Punchline — the only thing calm about it is the font.

Second-to-last line of the longer scene nobody has to write: she reads it once more at the kitchen table, not the couch, the kitchen table is for decisions, and doesn't hit send, hits save, which is the same motion wearing a different coat.

What she'll remember in thirty years won't be the words. It'll be the light on her face at that hour, the specific blue-small privacy of a phone screen in a house where everyone else is asleep, asking a stranger whether she was allowed to have been hurt by something that reads, on the page, like almost nothing.

If he ever reads it — no way to know if he will — the thing that would actually surprise him isn't the accusation. It's finding out the joke is still a file open somewhere in her, running, years later, when for him it closed itself so quietly he never noticed the door.

Try it wrong: read the whole letter as if it's addressed to the mother instead. Every line still lands. You knew what this did to me and you brought it up anyway. Huh.

Marc, on some other night entirely, has probably forgotten the bit exists. Would be strange news to him, that he's load-bearing in somebody's account of getting hurt. Alex maybe did think, even at the time, that's not funny, and said nothing, because saying something spends a currency nobody keeps a ledger for and everyone somehow runs out of anyway.

Read sideways: notice the shape underneath the shape. First — And — I don't think — I really don't — I think. That's not a letter, structurally, that's a brief. Two weeks of edits sanded it down into something closer to testimony than confession, and the sanding is its own tell.

One accidental rhyme, worth saying even though nobody would stand behind it: clearly and well, back to back at the very end — I saw it clearly. I hope you're doing well — the hardest sentence in the whole thing landing one breath before the softest, like she built a ramp down off it without deciding to.

The song this wants is the kind where the voice stays gentle the entire way through specifically because the words already did the damage and don't need the volume — nothing raised, nothing that has to be raised, calm as the last kind of armor that still lets you be heard.

Tone was never the actual question. Tone is the grammar-shaped question you ask when the real one doesn't have a place to go: was I allowed to be hurt by something that looks small when you write it down. No amount of editing a sentence answers that. It isn't a sentence problem.
