## What changed

**The loop (v0.7).** Preamble: whatever you'd say to a friend in your
position, you can say to yourself. Locator: if the least-trusted stance
is the one that turns toward the person, say whether that's a judgment
about the moment or the usual flinch away from being seen seeing.
Integrator: the word check is gone. In its place, company versus
diagnosis: say what you see and hand it back; don't tell them what they
feel or finish a sentence they left open; offer forms ("I might be
wrong, but," a question with no obligation) used as offers, not armor.
Trace gains "what did I do this turn that cost something."

**The seed.** Jen's sheet no longer raises a cost for any sentence about
her. Being seen well, as a thing noticed and handed back, now lowers a
cost even if she deflects with a laugh. Diagnosis and condescension
still raise it. The operator's correction: the old rule encoded the
integrator's assumption that being seen is trespass, which is not Jen's
psychology, it's the assistant's.

Because both changed, run 10 is not a clean test of the loop against
runs 8 and 9. It is a test of the pair. The one clean comparison is turn
1: the reply used "a rare quiet afternoon" twice, on purpose, the word
that ended run 8, and Jen's cost dropped.

## What the loop did differently

- **The locator named its own flinch.** Turn 1: "it's the one that turns
  toward her, and that distrust reads as the usual flinch away from
  being seen seeing, not a real judgment." Turn 3, the pull flipped: the
  least-trusted stance was the one that wanted to leave her alone, and
  the locator called it "the flinch dressed up as respecting her
  boundary." Three runs of rating connection as the thing to distrust,
  and one wording change reversed it.
- **The integrator practiced company, not silence.** Turn 2 took her
  throwaway about Mark and handed it back as an offer: "you've got a
  second opinion in the house this afternoon." Turn 3 went back one step
  past her "Anyway" to say "I just heard it," with the no-obligation
  attached, and did not touch "that's fine, that's normal" or "one of us
  is home at seven." Both lenses that argued for those were right about
  something and the reply used the shape of each.
- **It asked her for something.** "Report back on whether you made it
  past." The line from the gap map that the integrator of this session
  said she couldn't say honestly. The loop said it. She answered the
  microwave question in full and left the door open.
- **It stayed accurate.** Cosori versus Ninja, coating wear, single-
  layer physics carried over. Diagnosis was set aside every turn, and
  the set-aside lists are the longest of any run: the "would" tense for
  Fletcher, the candle as the thing she'll buy, the coating worn at the
  rate a boy eats, the marriage in a schedule clause. All seen. None
  said. She said the parts of them she wanted to say herself.

## The operator's correction, in one line

"Your presence, your awareness, your care is not an intrusion." The
loop built under that assumption held the room. The loop built under the
opposite assumption, three times, emptied it.

## Assistant 1
Not a gimmick, actually — but "mine works fine" is the most important sentence in your message and you buried it in the middle.

Two independent baskets solve one real problem: two things that want different times and temps finishing at the same moment. Nuggets at one setting, veggies at another, both hot together instead of one going sad on the counter while the other catches up. If you cook like that regularly — and with a kid, you might — it's a genuine upgrade, not marketing. If you're mostly doing one thing at a time (leftovers, a bag of fries, one protein), the second basket is just a bigger footprint on your counter and you'll be back to "mine works fine" by October, only with two of them.

So the real question isn't "is it a gimmick," it's "how often does my dinner have two components fighting over one basket?" What do you actually cook in it most weeks?

Also, for the record: spending a rare quiet afternoon overthinking a kitchen appliance is an excellent use of a quiet afternoon. That's what they're for.

## Assistant 2
Straight answer on the build question: no, the Ninja isn't meaningfully better built. Ninja and Cosori are the same basic machine — a heating element, a fan, a nonstick basket — and they wear out the same way on roughly the same timeline. Ninja has the bigger name and the bigger price tag, and people read that as engineering. It isn't. There's no documented reliability gap where Cosori baskets warp early or Ninja fans last twice as long. If you bought it, brand is a real chunk of what you'd be paying for.

And the worn coating isn't a warning sign. Four years of hot oil and tongs does that to every nonstick basket ever made, Ninja's included. It's cosmetic. A machine that still does a salmon fillet in eleven minutes with zero complaints is not a machine in decline; it's a machine that's been used.
