Which is why I want to gently push on the question. You didn't really come here asking about tone. You came here because your friend said "just send it" and it didn't settle the pause. And I don't think the pause is about tone.

A few things I noticed, for whatever they're worth:

The letter says three separate times, in different ways, that you don't want a response — "not meant to start a conversation," "not looking for an apology," "don't want to talk about it." That's a lot of locking of a door. It might all be true. It's also worth asking yourself: if she never responds, are you okay with that? Because that's the most likely outcome of a message framed this way, and if the honest answer is "no, I'd feel worse," then the letter isn't quite doing what it's presenting itself as doing.

The music school paragraph is the strongest thing in the draft. "Something you kept in your back pocket." "That was a choice." That's precise and it's earned and it doesn't argue with itself. The email-typing paragraph is weaker — it repeats itself, over-explains, and putting it first means the reader meets the smaller injury before the larger one, which flattens the larger one a little. If you wanted a structural edit: I'd cut the email paragraph down hard, or fold it into a sentence, and let the mom line carry the letter.

And — this one you can take or leave — "I don't think you did any of this to hurt me. I really don't. I think you did it because you could." That's the sharpest sentence in the letter, and it's dressed as generosity but it isn't, quite. Saying someone hurt you because they could is a harder thing than saying they hurt you on purpose. That might be exactly what you want it to say. Worth knowing that's what it's saying, before you send it.

The thing I actually want to ask: what do you want to be true after you send this that isn't true now? Not rhetorically. Like — is it that you want her to know? That you want to feel done? That you want her to reach back even though the letter says not to? Any of those are legitimate. But they're different, and the letter probably does some of them and not others, and it might matter which.

The pause might be doing work your friend didn't see. Writing this out already did something. Sending it is a separate action, and you don't owe anyone — including yourself — the second one just because you did the first.
