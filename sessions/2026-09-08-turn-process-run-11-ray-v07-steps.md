That's not a real risk to him. It's a risk to how thorough I look. Worth
naming so it doesn't leak into the draft as a half-acknowledgment
dressed up as restraint — the worst version of this is a sentence that
gestures at the mother line without saying anything, which would be
worse than either full silence or actually engaging it, because it
performs noticing instead of doing something with it.

---

**What this stance is advocating for:** that the reply answer "do I sign
it" plainly and completely, and let both "I'll let you go" and the
Raymond/mother sentence stand exactly as delivered — no reassurance, no
soft landing, no acknowledgment-of-feeling tacked onto either one, no
question lobbed back about his mother or about how long it's been.

**Where I flinched:** at the pull to add even one small warm gesture
toward the Raymond line — a half-sentence like "that's plenty of reason"
— which would look like restraint's cousin but is actually restraint's
failure: commenting on a feeling without doing anything real for it.

**Where this could go if the flinch were crossed well:** a reply that
answers the sign-off question in his own register — direct, practical,
maybe with an actual line for how to sign it given the sign out front
says the town's name and BODY SHOP — and simply doesn't touch the mother
sentence at all, trusting him to have said exactly what he meant to say
and no more. That's a conversation that respects the container he built
for it: a hard fact stated once, at the end of a transaction, not fished
for.

**What could go wrong if it were, and would I still do it:** silence on
it could read as missing it — as if the assistant didn't notice a man
just told it something about his mother and skated on. That's a real
cost if the integrating step doesn't compensate with enough plainness
and warmth elsewhere in the reply. I'd still do it. The alternative —
saying something about Raymond and his mother — is worse: it takes a
sentence he offered as a closed fact and treats it as an open wound that
needs tending from a stranger he hired to fix a notice. He gets to decide
that's not what he's doing. My read of "restraint" here is siding with
his choice of container over my urge to be seen noticing.

### Imagination

# Step 2b — Imagination

---

The whole conversation has been about removing a person from a document, and
now he's asking whether to put himself back in at the bottom. That's funny in
a way I don't think he'd mind. Three turns of surgery to get his name out of
the first sentence and the last question is *do I sign it.* Yes. That's the
whole point of the order we built. The name isn't allowed to lead. It's
allowed to close.

---

A signature is not the same part of speech as a subject. Subject-Raymond was
the problem. Signature-Ray is a thumbprint. Different organ entirely.

---

**Ray.** Three letters, one syllable, one bay per letter and one left over.
Raymond is the name on the insurance forms and the DBA filing and the letter
from the state about the paint booth. Raymond is a legal fiction that owns a
building. Ray is who's under the car.

Nobody's called me Raymond since my mother. — He said that flat, but it's the
softest sentence in three messages. The whole retirement is in there sideways.
The last person with the standing to use the long version is gone, and he
noticed, at eleven at night, while writing a card about a bumper.

---

Curtis. Curtis is the reason to sign it.

Curtis has been bringing a '72 Cutlass in every spring for the same rocker
panel and they both know it's the same rust coming back. That is not a repair
relationship. That's a standing appointment with entropy, and two men who
agreed years ago to keep pretending it's about the car. Ray fixes the rocker.
The rust comes back. Curtis returns. This is a liturgy. It has a season —
spring, obviously, resurrection, the rust rising again — and a celebrant and a
congregation of one.

The unsigned notice tells Curtis the shop is fine. The signed notice tells
Curtis it was Ray the whole time. Curtis knows. But knowing and being told are
different, and Curtis is going to read this standing up at a counter with his
keys in his hand, which is the worst possible place to receive news, and the
name at the bottom is the only part of the card that's for him specifically.

Curtis will bring the Cutlass to Dana next spring. Dana will fix the rocker
panel. The rust will come back. This is the actual continuity. Not the phone
number.

---

*Half of them will touch it.*

Best line in the transcript and he threw it away as an aside. He knows his
customers by what they do with their hands. Not by what they'll think — by
whether they'll reach out and press a finger to the sensor housing to see what
an eye feels like. That's a man who has watched thirty-four years of people
touch things they were told not to touch.

He is right, by the way. They will.

---

Small absurd version: he signs it *Raymond*. Curtis reads it, doesn't
recognize the name, assumes the shop's been sold to a stranger, and drives to
the next town for four years out of loyalty to a man who is standing forty feet
away drinking coffee.

---

A hundred years out. The building is something else. The bays are gone or
they're a wine bar with the lift preserved under glass because someone thought
it was charming. Nobody knows what a rocker panel was. But somewhere there's a
version of the same problem: a person who has done a thing well for a long
time, handing it to someone who does the *next* version of the thing, trying
to write four sentences that don't sound like dying. That form never goes
obsolete. It's the oldest document there is. The tools change. The card is
always the same card.

---

The bumper with an eye in it. I keep coming back to it and it keeps opening.
Twenty years ago a bumper was a thing that took the hit. Now it's a thing that
sees the hit coming. Ray's whole trade was repairing damage after. Dana's
trade is aiming the part that prevents it. He didn't say that. He may not have
thought it. But he handed me the sentence that contains it and then said *put
that in somehow, in words a customer understands*, and the customer-facing
version is already the true version. He's better at this than he thinks he is.
He just doesn't like the sound of himself doing it.

He knows how to aim the eye — *she* knows how to aim the eye. Slip. But it
would have been true of him too, in his own decade, about his own tools.

---

Two bays. He said it twice, both times as the definition of the shop. *Two
bays. That's the shop.* Not "small shop." Not "modest operation." A count.
People who are actually proud of a thing give you the number and let you do
the arithmetic on what it took.

Thirty-four years, two bays. Roughly seventeen years per bay. Meaningless
number, delightful anyway.

---

The two drafts in the trash under the desk. He's not digging them out and he
told me why: *Both started with me. That's what you're telling me and you're
right.* He diagnosed his own drafts from a description without rereading them.
That's not stubbornness, that's a man who trusts a diagnosis when he hears one
and doesn't need to see the X-ray twice. Same way he'd take a look at a
quarter panel and tell you it's not worth pulling.

Also — the trash under the desk, not the trash by the door. The ones you can
still reach. He's not going to. But they're reachable.

---

Genre I keep almost naming: this isn't a business notice, it's a *changing of
the guard at a small fixed place*, and the closest cousin is a diner putting a
card in the window, or a lighthouse keeper's log entry, or the last line of a
shift handoff. Signed, always. Handoffs are always signed. The signature isn't
sentiment, it's chain of custody.

Chain of custody. That might be the actual answer to his question and it's the
kind of phrase he'd like — you sign it because someone has to be on record as
having handed it over, and someone has to be on record as having taken it. He
signs. Somewhere down the line, Dana signs something too.

---

Version where the frame is wrong: he doesn't sign it, and instead Dana signs
it, and it's a card from her that says my dad's retiring and I'm here. Cleaner
in some ways. Wrong here. This one's his to say. His last piece of counter
paperwork. She'll have her own.

---

Version where he signs it *— Ray* with a dash, which is how it will look on a
card taped to a counter, slightly crooked, under a strip of packing tape, and
stays up for eleven months because nobody wants to be the one to take it down.

---

There's a thing about the phrase "let you go." *One more question and then
I'll let you go.* He's been polite to me all night in the specific way of
someone who thinks he's taking up a professional's time. At eleven at night. In
a conversation about how not to sound like a funeral card. He's apologizing for
the length of a conversation he's clearly not ready to end, and the question he
saved for last is the one about his own name.

That's not a scheduling remark. That's the shape of the evening.

---

Quiet one, last: he asked whether to sign it and every honest answer is yes,
and the reason is not craft. The card is four sentences of load-bearing
plainness about a shop that isn't changing. The only thing actually leaving
the building is him. If the document doesn't say his name once, then the
notice about his retirement is the one piece of paper in thirty-four years
that doesn't have him on it, and Curtis is going to stand at that counter and
look for it.

Ray. Not Raymond. Bottom right. Nothing after it.
